# **About the vault**

This an Obsidian vault contains my personal notes for preparation for the ETH entrance exams. So, naturally, two things follow:
1. Most of the information is relevant for these specific exams. However, it corresponds to most of the school programs with advanced maths, physics, biology, and chemistry.
2. However, not 100% of the information is relevant, nor is all the required information presented. As I composed these notes, I didn't feel the need to review every topic, so some are presented extremely concisely, while some of the topics are presented too elaborately, because I was simply interested in digging deeper

So, these notes are merely a starting point and a template. I tried to adapt the view for other people to be able to navigate around it, but I myself do not always use all of the features I added.

# **How is it built?**

The reason why I chose Obsidian over other methods of working with information is that here, I can present information not as a consecutive line, but as interconnected pieces. This is how I tried to construct the notes:
1. There is one ultimate note about one topic. It contains some statements about the topic. It is mostly focused on the main ideas, principles, and interconnections between different concepts.
2. Most of the terms in the text are linked to a file explaining the term.
3. The explanation file may also contain a specific example or lead elsewhere.
Of course, many of the notes do not follow this exact pattern. For example, there are places where I tried to create a solution template for maths and physics tasks.

There are several advantages to this method.  
First, the text is not overloaded with definitions, because they are basically outsourced.  
Secondly, one term often exists in different contexts. For example, when you see backlinks to ATP, you see all the processes in which it takes part. Then, when you have more neuronal connections with ATP, it is easier to remember what it is.  
Last but not least, it gives you the opportunity to first recognize the term in context and try to remember what it is, instead of simply reading the definition.

To each canvas I added some active recall zones. Lately, I have been trying to use this method, but I usually just forget or am too lazy and tired after studying to do it. So, to encourage myself and you, I added respective notes to each canvas. I find the best way to use it is to first write all the information you have in your head, then read the note with theory, then write in the same note all you can remember from the note.
# **Why are some notes so lame and how can you make them less lame?**

Apart from me being lazy and not writing a proper note (which is fixed by you adding the information yourself), there are a couple more things.

Sometimes I was lazy and wrote the note with AI. I do not advise it; it is the lamest of all lame, but I had little time.

Some subjects are impossible to explain in plain text, so the notes require plugins. Here is what I used:
1. LaTeX. There is a lot of it in maths and physics. It allows you to create a decent formula.
2. Spaced repetition. There are some notes marked with flashcards and a deck. They can be converted into flashcards by this plugin.
3. Ink. There are not many notes created with it, but I tried to make some templates for solutions. By the way, I encourage you to make more of such templates yourself.
4. Node collapse. Simply to make canvases less loaded.

I hope that someone will be able to adapt this format to view scientific knowledge the way I do.
